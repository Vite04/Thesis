\chapter{Discussion}\label{chap:discussion}
The results presented in the previous chapter allow the identification of consistent patterns in the interaction with IoT devices under different network configurations. In this chapter, these results are interpreted in relation to the research objectives and the background presented earlier, highlighting the role of the network perimeter, the reachability of the device under IPv6, and the conditions under which its services become externally accessible.

\section{Perimeter-Dependent Exposure as the Primary Risk Condition}\label{sec:discussion-perimeter-exposure}
A consistent observation across the experiment is that the external reachability of the device is determined not by the device itself, but by the filtering policy applied by the residential firewall. Between S1 and S2, the ESP32 maintained the same global IPv6 address, the same service configuration, the same default credentials, and the same firmware version. The only parameter that changed was the IPv6 residential router firewall state. Nonetheless, this change in the perimeter configuration resulted in two completely different exposure states.

As summarized in \tabref{tab:cross-scenario-comparative-results}, the cross-scenario comparison makes this asymmetry explicit. The ICMP response rate remains at 100\% and the EUI-64-derived MAC address is consistently observable across both scenarios, while port state, service accessibility, and inbound TCP connectivity differ entirely between S1 and S2. With the firewall enabled, ports 80 and 23 appeared as filtered, HTTP requests expired, and it was not possible to establish any Telnet session. Furthermore, the serial logs did not show any incoming TCP connections, indicating that the traffic was discarded before reaching the application layer. Once the firewall was disabled, both ports were reported as open, the dashboard and the hidden admin endpoint became reachable, and the Telnet service accepted the default credentials "admin/admin". As there was no modification in the host or its services, the observed differences can be attributed to the independent variable of the perimeter policy applied by the router.

The contrast observed between these scenarios indicates that, in residential IPv6 environments, exposure depends primarily on the perimeter configuration rather than representing an inherent vulnerability of IPv6 itself. Although IPv6 restores the architectural possibility of end to end connectivity through globally routable addressing, whether a device becomes externally reachable still depends on the filtering behavior of the residential router. The architecture enables the communication path, but the perimeter policy determines whether that path remains accessible from the Internet.

With this in mind, the expected security role of a residential IPv6 router is to enforce a restrictive default policy for unsolicited inbound traffic, particularly for TCP connections~\cite{rye2025firewalls}. The difference between S1 and S2 illustrates the practical meaning of this requirement. Under the restrictive policy, the device remained globally addressed but service inaccessible from the outside. When that filtering function was removed, the same address became a functional entry point to the device’s exposed services. 

This result is also relevant at larger scales beyond the residential setting. Prior measurements of dual stack networks have shown that IPv6 filtering policies can be more permissive than their IPv4 counterparts, including for high value services such as SSH, Telnet, and SNMP~\cite{czyz2016backdoor}. This suggests that the configuration gap observed in the experiment reflects a broader operational problem, where IPv6 connectivity is deployed without an equivalent security policy. Within the residential testbed, this condition was sufficient to expose weak internal services without requiring any modification to the device itself.

The key point is therefore not that IPv6 creates new vulnerabilities in the device itself, but that global addressing introduces an exposure vector through which pre-existing vulnerabilities become externally exploitable.

\section{ICMPv6 Reachability as a Persistent Discovery Vector}\label{sec:icmp-reachability}

The previous finding establishes the residential firewall as the decisive factor in determining whether global addressability translates into exposure at the application layer. However, S1 also reveals a more subtle distinction, blocking access at the application layer is not equivalent to eliminating external observability. Even when the firewall successfully prevented all inbound TCP connectivity, the device remained externally observable. In S1, HTTP requests expired, no Telnet session could be established, and the device registered no incoming application-layer connections. Nonetheless, the same device responded consistently to ICMPv6 probes sent from the external VPS, with 10/10 valid replies, zero packet loss, and stable RTT values (\tabref{tab:s1-external-ipv6-fw-enabled-results}). In S2, where TCP access was permitted, ICMPv6 behavior remained practically identical (\tabref{tab:s2-external-ipv6-fw-disabled-results}). The contrast between both scenarios shows that the firewall policy modified service accessibility, but not the ability of an external observer to verify that the host was active and reachable at the network layer.

This behavior is not necessarily the result of a misconfigured router, but rather a consequence of how IPv6 networking operates. ICMPv6 is not an auxiliary protocol that can be indiscriminately blocked without affecting network functionality. Unlike the common treatment of ICMP in IPv4 environments, IPv6 depends on ICMPv6 for normal operation. Neighbor Discovery, router discovery, and SLAAC rely directly on ICMPv6 exchanges, and the same process through which the ESP32 acquired its global IPv6 address depends on these mechanisms. Blocking ICMPv6 indiscriminately at the network boundary would interfere with normal IPv6 operation, including connectivity diagnostics and path MTU discovery, which is why selective filtering that preserves essential protocol functionality is the recommended approach~\cite{rfc4890}. From this perspective, the fact that the device remained pingable even behind a correctly configured firewall does not necessarily indicate misconfiguration, but rather a predictable consequence of a functional IPv6 deployment.

This result becomes especially relevant when considering the addressing properties discussed in \secref{sec:results-addressing}. The global IPv6 address assigned to the ESP32 embeds the MAC address directly in the interface identifier through EUI-64, making the address stable and manufacturer-identifiable~\cite{rye2023ipvseeyou}. As a result, an external observer receiving a valid ICMPv6 reply not only confirms that the host is active, but can also derive the hardware address and identify the manufacturer through the OUI prefix without requiring additional interaction. Under S1 conditions, the exposed services remained inaccessible, but the host itself was still externally identifiable through a stable, hardware-derived network identity.

S1 reveals a separation between two conditions that are often treated as equivalent, discoverability and exploitability. The firewall reduced the attack surface by preventing access to HTTP and Telnet, but it did not remove the device from external visibility. If an external actor already knows the IPv6 address of the host, or is able to infer it through target generation techniques, a single ICMPv6 probe is sufficient to confirm that the system exists, responds, and remains active. In this sense, the firewall operates as a barrier against direct exploitation, but not as a mechanism that eliminates host discoverability. Candidate IPv6 addresses generated through adaptive target-generation techniques can still be validated through probing even when service-level access remains blocked~\cite{williams20246sense}.

S1 therefore represents an observable host whose services remain inaccessible from outside the residential network. S2, by contrast, represents the same host under a perimeter configuration where access to the application layer becomes possible. The difference between both scenarios is not the presence of the device in the network, but whether that presence translates into effective interaction with its exposed services. The residential firewall successfully blocked direct access to HTTP and Telnet, but it did not eliminate the external visibility of the host. It should therefore be understood as a necessary mechanism for containing direct exploitation, but insufficient for eliminating external detectability. This gap between what the firewall prevents and what it is often assumed to prevent connects directly with the broader conditions examined in the following section.

\section{The End of Security by Obscurity}

Residential IoT deployments have often relied on the assumption of limited direct reachability from the public Internet. This assumption results from two independent processes under different protocol conditions, each associated with a different form of obscurity. In IPv4, obscurity is architectural and produced by NAT, where devices do not have publicly routable addresses. In IPv6, obscurity is assumed to be computational, based on the size and structure of the address space, where devices are globally addressable but expected to be difficult to discover.

Both forms of obscurity are reflected in scenarios S2 and S4. Under IPv4 (S4), the external VPS reached the public address of the residential router, but no service on the ESP32 was accessible. All device functionality remained confined to the local network. Under IPv6 (S2), the same device became directly reachable once the firewall was disabled, exposing HTTP endpoints and a Telnet service without any modification to the device itself, as shown in \tabref{tab:s2-external-ipv6-fw-disabled-results} and \tabref{tab:s4-ipv4-baseline-results}. The difference is not the device, but the conditions under which it is exposed.

Under IPv4/NAT, this containment is produced by the network architecture itself. Since no mapping exists for unsolicited inbound traffic, inbound connections are discarded by default~\cite{rfc3022}. Under IPv6, this implicit containment disappears once the device acquires a globally routable address. From that point, whether the services remain reachable from the Internet depends on the filtering policy applied by the residential router. As observed in S2, removing that filtering policy was sufficient to expose all running services without any modification to the device itself.

A second form of obscurity is associated with the size and structure of the IPv6 address space. Even though IPv6 devices are globally addressable, it is often assumed that large scale host discovery remains impractical due to the size of the address space itself. The observations discussed in \secref{sec:icmp-reachability} show that this assumption does not fully hold. Even when service-level access remained blocked, the device continued responding to ICMPv6 probes, while the EUI-64 addressing mechanism introduced predictable structure into the interface identifier~\cite{rye2023ipvseeyou}. At the same time, adaptive target-generation techniques have demonstrated that active IPv6 hosts can still be discovered through learned address patterns, while network structure can be inferred through side-channel methods without exhaustive enumeration~\cite{williams20246sense,pan2023router}. In this sense, the size of the IPv6 address space alone is not sufficient to prevent external discovery.

The results discussed throughout this section show that neither form of obscurity remains sufficient under the conditions examined in this thesis. The device is no longer indirectly protected by the absence of a publicly reachable address, nor can it rely on address-space size alone to remain difficult to discover. The transition examined here is therefore not a change in the internal security properties of the device itself, but the progressive removal of external conditions that had historically limited its exposure. This is the shift reflected in the title of the thesis, from security by obscurity to imminent exposure.

\section{Weak Services as Exposure Amplifiers}\label{sec:discussion-weak-services}

Once the device becomes externally reachable, the behavior of the exposed services becomes directly relevant to the security state of the system. The experiment does not only evaluate whether the ESP32 can be reached from outside the residential network, but also what the device exposes once that access becomes possible. Under these conditions, the implemented HTTP and Telnet services offered no meaningful resistance once inbound traffic reached the device.

These weaknesses were deliberately implemented for the purposes of the experiment, but they were not arbitrary. S2 confirmed that all three became directly accessible once inbound traffic was permitted (\tabref{tab:s2-external-ipv6-fw-disabled-results}, \appref{app:results-s2}). As discussed in \chpref{chap:background}, IoT security research has repeatedly documented devices that expose weak application-layer controls, provide management interfaces over HTTP, retain legacy administrative services such as Telnet, or include undocumented functionality outside the normal user interface. The firmware used in this experiment does not attempt to reproduce every possible IoT device, nor to emulate a specific commercial product. Instead, it implements controlled weakness patterns that have already been observed in real embedded deployments~\cite{alrawi2019sok,costin2014firmware,liu2025eagleye}. The corresponding firmware implementation, including the HTTP and Telnet service logic, is documented in \appref{app:source-code}.

The Telnet service represents the clearest example of this weakness pattern. Plaintext administrative access and default credentials have repeatedly appeared in large scale IoT compromise, most notably in the Mirai botnet~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. The credential pair \texttt{admin/admin} used in this experiment reflects the same type of weak authentication mechanism exploited in those attacks. S3 additionally showed that this weakness was not limited to remote access. Within the local network, the Telnet authentication exchange, including prompts and credentials transmitted in plaintext, was directly observable in Wireshark captures without any decryption process (\tabref{tab:s3-local-passive-observation-results} and \appref{app:results-s3}).

The broader implication is that exposure does not create the weakness itself, but changes the conditions under which that weakness becomes relevant. Under the IPv4 conditions observed in S4, plaintext Telnet, weak authentication, and the hidden administrative endpoint remained confined to the local network. Under S2, the same services became directly reachable from outside the residential environment. The device itself did not become less secure between scenarios. What changed was whether those weaknesses remained locally contained or became externally accessible through the network perimeter.

For this reason, weak services act as exposure amplifiers. The permissive IPv6 perimeter does not create the insecure HTTP endpoint, the hidden administrative interface, or the default Telnet credentials. What changes is the scope under which those weaknesses operate. Services that would otherwise remain locally contained become remotely reachable once the device acquires a globally routable address and the residential router permits inbound traffic.

\section{Validity and Scope of the Findings}\label{sec:discussion-validity-scope}

The experimental results are supported by a set of conditions that remained consistent across all scenarios. Between S1 and S2, the firewall state was the only parameter that changed, while the device, firmware version, service configuration, and measurement tools remained unchanged throughout the experiment. S0 fulfilled its role as an internal baseline by verifying that the HTTP and Telnet services were operational before any external interaction was introduced. This condition is important because the contrast between S1 and S2 depends on the device remaining functional before the perimeter configuration changed. The observed differences between both scenarios can therefore be attributed to the residential filtering policy rather than to inconsistencies at the device level. Additional validation was provided through the ESP32 serial logs, which recorded inbound connections, requested resources, and authentication events across the different scenarios. This allowed the external observations to be compared directly with the events registered by the device itself. The measurement criteria defined in \secref{sec:measurement-evaluation}, including ICMPv6 reachability, TCP port state, HTTP and Telnet accessibility, and traffic visibility, were applied consistently throughout the experiment, and the observations remained coherent across the collected evidence within each scenario.

Several constraints limit the external validity of the experiment. First, the evaluation was conducted using a single embedded device running custom firmware on the lwIP network stack. Although the implemented services and configuration patterns were intentionally based on weakness patterns documented in the IoT literature (\chpref{chap:background}), the observations cannot be assumed to represent all firmware implementations, device categories, or hardware architectures. Second, the residential router used in the experiment corresponds to a specific model provided by a Swiss ISP. The observed firewall behavior, including the fact that ICMPv6 probes received valid replies while inbound TCP connections remained blocked in S1, reflects the implementation decisions of that particular device. Different residential routers may apply different default filtering policies, stateful inspection behavior, or ICMPv6 handling under equivalent configurations. Third, all external measurements were performed from a single VPS located in a European datacenter and during a limited observation period. The measured RTT values and connectivity behavior therefore reflect that specific routing context and may differ under alternative network paths, geographic locations, or upstream filtering conditions.

Although the experimental setup reflects a realistic and reproducible residential IPv6 deployment using real commercial hardware and infrastructure, the results should be interpreted as evidence of a broader class of risk conditions rather than as a universal characterization of all residential IPv6 environments. More specifically, the experiment examines how perimeter configuration influences the external exposure of IoT services under globally routable addressing. Within this scope, the central observation is not that all IPv6 residential networks expose IoT devices, but that under the tested conditions a single change in the residential filtering policy was sufficient to move the same device from local availability to external service accessibility.
