\chapter{Limitations and Mitigation}\label{chap:limitations-mitigation}

\section{Limitations}

Beyond the methodological limitations discussed in the previous section, several broader limitations remain. These do not affect the internal consistency of the observations reported in Chapter~4, but rather delimit the scope under which the findings should be interpreted.

The residential firewall was evaluated under two states only, enabled and disabled. This was enough to isolate the perimeter as the primary exposure variable, but it does not capture the intermediate filtering policies that may be present in residential environments, such as selective TCP filtering, ISP-defined configurations, per-source rate limiting, or stateful inspection rules tied to outbound flows~\cite{rye2025firewalls}. The contrast summarized in Table~4.7 therefore reflects the behavior of a real residential deployment under two specific perimeter conditions, but should not be interpreted as covering the full range of filtering behavior that may exist across residential environments.

The external VPS reached the ESP32 by using the global IPv6 address recorded directly from the device serial output (Section~4.1, Appendix~B.1), meaning that the host address was already known before the external interaction began. The experiment therefore evaluates reachability under known-address conditions rather than the process through which a host becomes discoverable. Whether the same device would have been identified through adaptive scanning techniques~\cite{williams20246sense} or inferred through side-channel observations~\cite{pan2023router} under the same residential configuration was not evaluated within the experimental setup, although both approaches were discussed in the literature reviewed in Section~2.4. The observations therefore describe the conditions under which external access becomes possible after host identification, rather than the complete path from discovery to service access.

The scope of the threat model remains bounded to three observable conditions, network reachability, service accessibility, and traffic visibility. The scenarios showed that the HTTP root endpoint, the hidden administrative interface, and the Telnet service became directly reachable from the external VPS under S2, and that authentication with the default credentials succeeded (Table~4.4, Appendix~B.3). However, the experiment did not extend beyond the point of successful access toward actions such as persistence, lateral movement, or remote code execution. The observations therefore describe the conditions that enable an external host to reach the application layer, rather than the complete impact that may follow after access has been obtained. Prior work has documented the consequences of comparable access conditions at larger scale~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai,alrawi2019sok,liu2025eagleye}, whereas the present experiment evaluates the entry condition rather than the full sequence of exploitation events.

 The experiment also remains bounded to a specific IPv6 deployment model. The IPv4 comparison performed in S4 uses a native dual-stack residential connection, in which the same router provides a public static IPv4 address alongside native IPv6 (Section~3.2, Table~3.1). The experiment does not address alternative deployment models such as carrier-grade NAT combined with IPv6, IPv6-only access with NAT64, or tunneling-based transition mechanisms. These configurations may introduce different perimeter conditions and should not be assumed to reproduce the IPv6/IPv4 contrast observed in this work.

\section{Network-Level Mitigations}
Within the scope defined above, the clearest mitigation target is the residential perimeter. Although the experiment does not characterize all possible IPv6 deployment models or filtering policies, the S1/S2 contrast (Section~5.1, Table~4.7) shows that the firewall state alone was enough to move the same device from external service inaccessibility to full external reachability. Mitigation at this layer is therefore the most immediate way to reduce exposure, since it does not require any modification of the device, the firmware, or the IPv6 protocol itself.

The first and most immediate mitigation concerns the filtering policy applied at that perimeter. The expected baseline behavior of a residential IPv6 router is to discard unsolicited inbound traffic by default and to allow it only in response to explicit configuration or to outbound-initiated flows~\cite{rfc6092}. This behavior is shown in S1, where the router blocked inbound TCP traffic toward ports 23 and 80 despite the device being globally addressed (Table~4.3, Appendix~B.2). However, the observations suggest that the relevant objective is not simply enabling or disabling the firewall as a whole, but controlling which devices and services remain externally reachable. A device intended to be accessible from outside the residential network should be exposed through a specific and deliberate rule, in the same way that an IPv4 deployment exposes an internal host through an explicit port-forwarding entry rather than by removing NAT entirely (Section~3.2). Under IPv6, this selective exposure remains architecturally possible and forms part of the recommended residential security model~\cite{rfc6092}, but it depends on the availability of filtering controls at the level of individual devices and services rather than a single global switch. 

The practical difficulty is that this level of granularity is not always available. Measurements of residential deployments document variations in default configurations and inbound filtering behavior~\cite{rye2025firewalls}. Similar differences have also been observed in organizational networks, where IPv6 policies remain more permissive than their IPv4 counterparts even for protocols such as SSH and Telnet~\cite{czyz2016backdoor}. This observation is particularly relevant because filtering behavior in IPv4 should not be assumed to transfer automatically to IPv6. This is visible in the experiment itself: the same services remained unreachable under IPv4/NAT (S4, Table~4.6) but became directly accessible under IPv6 once the corresponding filtering policy was not enforced (S2, Table~4.4). When the only available control consists of a binary firewall state, selective exposure becomes difficult to achieve. The perimeter either remains fully restrictive, which also blocks legitimate inbound services, or becomes broadly permissive, reproducing the conditions observed in S2. In this context, the responsibility extends beyond the operator of the device itself and depends heavily on the default configurations and control mechanisms provided by router manufacturers and ISPs.

A second consideration applies to the treatment of ICMPv6 at the network boundary. The observation discussed in Section~5.2 that the ESP32 remained pingable under S1 does not imply that all ICMPv6 traffic should be discarded. ICMPv6 forms part of the normal operation of IPv6 itself, since Neighbor Discovery, address resolution, and Path MTU Discovery depend on it~\cite{rfc4861,rfc4443}, and indiscriminate filtering would interfere with the same SLAAC process through which the ESP32 acquired its global address (Section~4.1). A more appropriate approach is selective filtering, where message types required for protocol functionality are preserved while non-essential ICMPv6 traffic originating from outside the network can be filtered or rate-limited~\cite{rfc4890}. Under such conditions, the simplest mechanism for externally confirming host activity can be reduced, although adaptive scanning and indirect inference techniques may still identify active hosts without relying on echo responses~\cite{pan2023router,williams20246sense}.

\section{Device-Level Mitigations}

While the previous paragraph addresses how external traffic reaches the device, the present section focuses on what the device reveals about itself once it participates in IPv6 communication. The two operate on different dimensions of exposure. The first determines whether the device can be reached, while the second determines what remains observable even when service access is restricted. The findings presented in Section~4.1 show that the ESP32 derived its interface identifier directly from its hardware MAC address through the EUI-64 mechanism, producing a stable and manufacturer-identifiable global IPv6 address (Table~4.1). This identifier remained unchanged across all scenarios and remained observable even under the most restrictive perimeter condition tested, since the ICMPv6 responses recorded in S1 (Appendix~B.2) carried the same EUI-64-derived address despite all TCP traffic being blocked. As discussed in Section~5.2, this means that perimeter filtering alone does not prevent the disclosure of hardware-derived addressing information. An external observer holding a candidate address may still infer manufacturer-attributable information regardless of whether service-level access is available~\cite{rye2023ipvseeyou}. Application-layer weaknesses such as those examined in Section~5.4 require their own firmware-level mitigations, but these concerns are not specific to the IPv6 transition addressed by this work and have already been extensively documented elsewhere~\cite{costin2014firmware,alrawi2019sok}.

The mitigation therefore lies at the addressing layer of the device itself. Two complementary mechanisms have been standardized. Stable but semantically opaque interface identifiers preserve addressability without embedding hardware-derived information~\cite{rfc7217}, while temporary interface identifiers rotate over time and add temporal unlinkability to outbound communications~\cite{rfc8981}. The two are not mutually exclusive, since a device may use a stable opaque address for incoming connections while generating temporary addresses for outbound flows. The lwIP-based stack used in this work implements neither mechanism and falls back to EUI-64 by default, which produced the address structure observed in Table~4.1. The mitigation therefore depends on the firmware author and on the network stack they choose. The residential perimeter cannot modify how interface identifiers are generated, and application-layer hardening does not alter addressing decisions made beneath it.

\section{The Limits of End-User Responsibility}

The mitigations discussed previously in this chapter are addressed primarily to ISPs, router manufacturers, and IoT device manufacturers. This allocation reflects the fact that the variables that determined the exposure observed in this work were largely outside the practical control of the end-user of the device.

This limitation begins with what the user can observe. From inside the residential network, the device may appear to behave identically whether the perimeter is filtering external traffic or not. The firewall state changes what an external observer sees (S1 vs.~S2, Section~5.1, Table~4.7), but it does not necessarily change how the device behaves toward local hosts. An end user without access to external infrastructure therefore has no straightforward way to determine whether their device is reachable from the public Internet or what it exposes there. Measurements of the residential IPv6 ecosystem confirm that permissive default policies occur in real CPE configurations~\cite{rye2025firewalls}, but the user has no built-in mechanism to verify which configuration applies to them.

Even when the situation is recognized, the controls available to the user do not match the structure of the exposure. External service reachability, ICMPv6-level discoverability, and EUI-64-based identifiability arise from different mechanisms and remain present under different conditions (Sections~5.1, 5.2). The binary IPv6 firewall control exposed by the testbed router reduces these dimensions to a single decision and does not address EUI-64 identifiability at all, since the addressing behavior of the device is determined by the network stack and remains outside any policy applied at the perimeter.

What remains within the user's reach is bounded and indirect. Keeping firmware updated, avoiding the explicit activation of features that expose the device beyond the local network when not required, and isolating IoT devices on a separate segment when the router supports it are reasonable practices, but they would not have altered the S1/S2 contrast. The decisive variables remained in the firmware and in the router defaults, and ordinary user action could not directly change either layer.

This does not mean that end users are exempt from operational responsibility, but that their position is structural. They inherit an exposure baseline they did not design, they lack the visibility to evaluate it, and they lack the controls to refine it. Any framework that intends to reduce the exposure observed in this work across the broader residential ecosystem must therefore begin with the actors that determine those baselines, in line with the mitigations discussed for network operators (Section~6.2) and device manufacturers (Section~6.3).
