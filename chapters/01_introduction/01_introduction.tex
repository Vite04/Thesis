\chapter{Introduction}
This thesis explores how global IPv6 addressing increases the attack surface of the Internet of Things (IoT) environment. The study combines a comprehensive literature review and an empirical assessment of default system configurations through controlled laboratory demonstrations, highlighting how the adoption of new network standards can potentially affect the security of millions of end IoT devices.

The IPv4 to IPv6 transition is the result of an inherent limitation in the design of IPv4’s address space, which only supports $2^{32}$ unique addresses. A boundary already exceeded by the estimated 18.8 billion IoT devices in 2024~\cite{iotanalytics2024}. IPv6 mitigates this limitation by implementing a 128-bit architecture, offering a virtually infinite address space. Despite its technical advantage, the global adoption has been gradual due to the current scale of IPv4-based infrastructures and the cost of migration. Therefore, technologies such as Network Address Translation (NAT) were adopted to extend the lifetime of IPv4~\cite{rfc3022}.

In practice, NAT allows multiple internal hosts within a private local-area network (LAN) to share a single public IPv4 address by translating internal IP addresses and TCP/UDP port numbers to a public address and port at the network boundary. This translation process is stateful and connection-oriented, meaning that the address mappings are created only for outbound traffic initiated by hosts within the LAN~\cite{rfc3022,rfc4787}. Consequently, unless explicitly allowed through static port-forwarding rules, any unsolicited inbound traffic that does not match an existing mapping entry is discarded by default ~\cite{rfc4787}. Nonetheless, the use of NAT translation tables violates the original end-to-end connectivity principle of the Internet architecture~\cite{saltzer1984end}, and simultaneously provides a form of perimeter protection by preventing direct external access to internal hosts. 

The loss of NAT and increased IoT exposure reflect a shift in network architecture paradigms. Until now, many IoT devices have been designed under an implicit threat model that assumes limited inbound reachability, relying primarily on network topology, firewall configurations, and traffic routing rather than on end-device security mechanisms. With the absence of default NAT-based containment in IPv6, this assumption no longer holds. Depending on the network configuration, devices become directly reachable without any change to their internal security properties, thereby increasing their effective exposure~\cite{saltzer1984end,rfc8200, rye2025firewalls}.

The direct exposure of IoT devices is especially problematic due to the attack vectors commonly associated with this ecosystem. A large number of IoT devices have exposed services that are only protected by weak or default authentication mechanisms, enabling large-scale automated exploitation~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai}. Embedded devices often contain insecure network services or misconfigurations, as well as persistent vulnerabilities produced by design constraints and limited update mechanisms~\cite{costin2014firmware}. Poorly secured management interfaces and API calls significantly expand the attack surface when IoT devices become externally reachable~\cite{fernandes2016smarthome}. It is precisely the interaction between inherent device vulnerabilities and expanded reachability that increases the risk of remote exploitation.

\section{Research Objectives}
The main objective of this thesis is to analyze how the transition to globally routable IPv6 addressing increases the attack surface of IoT devices by enabling direct external exposure in real-world network environments. Specifically, this study aims to identify under which router configurations, network parameters, and default device settings IoT devices become directly accessible from external networks. 

\section{Contributions}
This thesis contributes a reproducible residential IPv6 testbed for studying IoT exposure under controlled conditions, combining an ESP32 device running custom firmware, a residential Internet service provider (ISP) router, an internal observer, and an external IPv6-enabled virtual private server (VPS). The design isolates the residential firewall state as the primary independent variable and operates on a real access network rather than an emulated environment.
Using this setup, the thesis provides empirical evidence that, in the tested setting, external service exposure is determined primarily by the residential perimeter rather than by the device itself. It further distinguishes service accessibility from host observability, showing that a device may remain externally observable and hardware-identifiable even when the perimeter blocks inbound service traffic. The findings are then situated within the broader notion of security by obscurity, separating the architectural obscurity associated with IPv4/NAT from the computational obscurity associated with the IPv6 address space.

\section{Thesis Structure}
Chapter 2 provides the necessary technical background on IPv4, IPv6, residential network architectures, and IoT security models. Chapter 3 describes the experimental methodology and testbed used to evaluate device exposure under different network configurations. Chapter 4 presents the empirical results obtained across the evaluated scenarios. Chapter 5 discusses these findings and examines their implications for IoT exposure in private IPv6 environments. Chapter 6 addresses the limitations of the study and discusses mitigation strategies at the network, device, and end-user levels. Finally, Chapter 7 concludes the thesis by summarizing the main findings and outlining possible directions for future research.
